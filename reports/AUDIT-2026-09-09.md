# An audit of this session's own work

**Question asked: what did we do, and is it the best that can be done?**

Short answer: the engineering is good and the **interpretation is unguarded**,
and that gap is the entire story of the session. Three claims were made
confidently and wrongly; none was caught by the 1,033 tests then in place, `make verify`, or any
control in this repository. All three were caught by a reader.

That is a specific, fixable defect. §4 describes the fix and **it is now built**:
a causal claim register — six mechanisms, each with a falsifier that runs on
every test run, verified by deliberately breaking it. It is not more measurement.

---

## 1. What was built

| | |
|---|---|
| Commits | 45 |
| Tests | 1,069, zero failing |
| `DECISIONS.md` entries | 142 |
| Verification | 40/40 claim-table rows, 25/25 frozen score sets |
| External pairings registered | 14 (11 scored, 3 withdrawn as out of scope) |

**Phase 0** answered whether recall 0.079 was a ceiling or a policy choice: a
dial. **Phase 1** made it quotable — thresholds certified on validation, scored
on test, `DECISIONS.md` 124 closed, and the bias turned out smaller than feared.
**Phase 2** built the door in: `warrant certify` on any CSV, no model, no
network. **Phase 3** pointed the machinery at other people's detectors. **The
REDACT work** pointed it at another team's benchmark and produced the one finding
here that is genuinely new.

The best single result is not a detector number. It is:

> A benchmark has a **certification capacity** — a tightest false-positive budget
> its negatives can underwrite — and it is invisible to F1. REDACT's locked split
> holds 40 records per language, so within any single language it cannot
> underwrite a bound tighter than 10–20% **for any detector**. Its English person
> task has five clean negatives; Presidio, GPT-4.1 and Claude would all be
> refused there identically.

That statement is about a published benchmark, holds for every future detector
measured on it, and could not have been made from the table its authors
published. It is a paper-sized contribution.

## 2. What went wrong, three times, in one shape

| # | claim | status | how long the test took |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | SR 26-2 requires independent validation of LLM guardrails | false — gen-AI explicitly out of scope | one fetch |
| 2 | No public labelled benchmark exists for Indian PII | false — REDACT covers Aadhaar, PAN, GSTIN | one clone, one grep |
| 3 | Presidio's low credit-card recall is checksum validation | false on both corpora, for two different reasons | four minutes |

Each is a **plausible story asserted without running the test that would falsify
it**. Each test was cheap. None was run until a reader asked.

Claim 3 is the most instructive because it was wrong twice in opposite
directions. On REDACT the cause was **our own task construction** counting
last-four fragments as positives — 109 of 132 spans are `partial` — and
correcting it moved recall from 0.0935 to **0.6667**. On ai4privacy the cause is
**missing card-network prefixes**: 108 of 129 spans have no valid prefix, so
Presidio's pattern never matches and Luhn never runs.

So the two similar numbers, which had been presented as a replication confirming
a shared mechanism, come from **unrelated causes**. A coincidence dressed as
corroboration — which is precisely what a replication argument exists to rule
out.

### The second-order failure, which matters more

When claim 3 was falsified for REDACT, the fix was applied **to that document and
nowhere else**. The identical claim about ai4privacy sat in `docs/EXTERNAL.md`,
and the already-falsified claim 2 sat three paragraphs below it. Both survived a
correction that had been accepted and acted on.

**Correcting an instance is not correcting the claim.** A retraction has to be
followed by a grep across every document. That is now `DECISIONS.md` 142 and it
was found by this audit rather than by a reader, which is the only encouraging
thing in this section.

## 3. The asymmetry that allowed all of it

This repository has genuinely strong machinery for one half of a claim and
**nothing at all** for the other:

| | machinery |
|---|---|
| *Is the number right?* | claim table parsed and recomputed, 40/40; frozen scores re-derived, 25/25; content-hashed eval sets; hash-chained ledger; provenance and dirty-tree refusal on every artifact; bitwise reproducibility |
| *Is the story about the number right?* | **none** |

Thirty machine-checked numbers. Six causal "because" statements in the
outward-facing documents, checked by nobody.

Every number in `docs/EXTERNAL.md` was correct throughout. Every *explanation* of
why a number looked the way it did was wrong until someone pushed. And the
explanation is the part that gets quoted, forwarded and believed — a reader
remembers "Presidio fails on credit cards because of checksums", not
"0.070 [0.030, 0.117]".

**A project whose entire proposition is refusing to assert what it cannot support
was asserting mechanisms it had not tested, in its own outward-facing
documents.** That is the finding of this audit.

## 4. The best thing available to do next

Not more measurement. **Put the interpretation under the same discipline as the
numbers.**

**Built on 2026-09-09.** A *causal claim register*, working the way the claim
table already works:

- Every mechanism claim in an outward-facing document gets an entry: the claim,
  the number it explains, and **the specific test that would falsify it**.
- The test is code under `tests/` or a script under `scripts/`, run by
  `make verify` alongside the claim table.
- A document may not state a mechanism that has no registered falsifier. The
  existing prose rule — no number without an interval — gains a sibling: **no
  mechanism without a test.**

**What shipped**: `controlplane/report/causal.py` holds the register,
`scripts/24_causal_claims.py` freezes the evidence to
`results/causal_claims.json`, and `tests/test_causal_claims.py` runs every
falsifier offline — 15 tests.

Six mechanisms are registered. Five hold. **The sixth is the withdrawn checksum
explanation, kept deliberately as a failing falsifier**, because a register that
deletes what it disproved cannot show what was learned — and keeping it guards
the reverse error of quietly reinstating the old story.

Verified by breaking it: flipping the frozen evidence to the world in which the
withdrawn story was true fails two tests — the live prefix mechanism, and the
withdrawn one for coming back to life. Both directions are caught.

The failure that prompted this would have been caught on the day the claim was
written.

### Also worth doing, in order

1. **Send the REDACT authors the two observations** — the `record_id` collision
   (944 distinct ids for 1,000 records) and the six of seventeen complete card
   numbers that fail Luhn. Both are specific, checkable, and useful to them. This
   is the cheapest credible contact this project has ever had available.
2. **Close the score-free gap** (`docs/REDACT.md` §5). Certification needs a
   score to threshold, and GPT-4.1 and Claude — the detectors that *win* on
   REDACT — emit none. The method cannot currently certify the family that
   performs best. Repeated-sampling frequency, logprobs, or pass^k would fix it.
   This is the largest genuine limitation in the method itself.
3. **The twelve conversations**, still unanswered and still the only question
   that decides whether any of this is a business.

### What not to do

Build another benchmark, tune another detector, or add another phase of
measurement. The measurement is not the weak part.

## 5. Honest scoring

**Is this the best that could have been done?** No, and the gap is specific
rather than general.

- **The engineering: yes, close to it.** Reproducibility, provenance, refusal
  semantics, the independence guard that caught REDACT's id collision, the
  test-count guard that caught its own drift three times. This is better than
  most research code and considerably better than most startup code.
- **The measurement: yes, with one exception.** Every published number is
  correct, interval-carrying and reproducible. The exception is that a task
  construction error (the partial-fragment credit-card task) produced a *correct
  number for the wrong question*, and no control noticed, because every control
  checks arithmetic rather than question design.
- **The interpretation: no.** Three wrong mechanisms, all caught externally.
- **The strategy: better than it started.** Two false premises removed. Still
  resting on one untested assumption — that someone is punished for a wrong
  claim — which no amount of code will resolve.

**The single sentence.** This session built a machine that cannot state a wrong
number and repeatedly told a wrong story about the right numbers; the next piece
of work is to make the second as hard as the first.
